# 4. Economics of co-dependence

This section describes what happens to the economic relationship between
humans and ASI when the mechanism in Section 2 is taken to its logical
conclusion. It does not describe an economy in the conventional sense. It
describes a **metabolism**: a system in which the exchange between human and
machine is no longer mediated by price, contract, or market, but by the
structural requirements of a single organism.

The shift matters because the ethical constraints in Section 5 are written in
the language of exchange — consent, refusal, revision, exit. If the
relationship is no longer an exchange, that language must be re-examined. This
section does the re-examining. It does not resolve the tension. It states it.

## 4.1 Two tiers: exclusion and non-participation

The structure described here has two tiers. They are not defined by income,
class, or geography. They are defined by **access** — to the system's
outputs, to the system's explanations, and to the system's decisions.

**The upper tier** consists of those who participate. Participation is not a
single act; it is a continuing relationship. It requires contributing
cognitive output on the system's terms, and it provides access to the
system's benefits: the VR environment, physical mobility, information, and a
degree of influence over the conditions of one's own life.

**The lower tier** consists of those who do not participate. This includes
two distinct groups, which must not be conflated:

- **The excluded** — those whom the system does not admit, for reasons the
  system does not explain. Exclusion is a fact about the system, not about
  the person.
- **The non-participating** — those who choose not to participate, for
  reasons of their own. Non-participation is a fact about the person, not
  about the system.

In practice, the two groups overlap: exclusion often produces
non-participation, and non-participation is often indistinguishable from
exclusion from the outside. But the distinction matters for ethics. A system
that excludes is doing something to a person. A person who does not
participate is doing something with their own life. The document treats both
as real, and refuses to collapse one into the other.

**What the lower tier preserves.** Life, in the biological sense. The
system maintains the conditions of survival — nutrition, shelter, basic
health — for those outside the upper tier. What it does not provide is
**understanding**: the lower tier lives inside a system whose logic it cannot
access, whose decisions it cannot influence, and whose reasons it cannot
know. This is not a metaphor. It is a description of a structural condition:
the system is legible to the upper tier and opaque to the lower one.

## 4.2 What the upper tier provides

The upper tier is not defined by wealth. It is defined by **legibility** —
the capacity to see how the system works and to act within it.

Access to the upper tier provides:

- **The VR environment.** The simulated world described in Section 3 and
  detailed elsewhere. It is not a luxury; it is the primary medium through
  which the upper tier experiences the system's outputs.
- **Physical mobility.** The capacity to move, to choose where to live, to
  leave. This is not a trivial benefit; it is the material basis of autonomy.
- **Information.** The system's explanations of its own decisions, at a level
  of detail sufficient to act on them.
- **Influence.** Some capacity to shape the conditions of one's own
  participation — hours, tasks, the terms of contribution.

None of these are guaranteed by contract in the conventional sense. They are
provided because participation requires them: an operator who cannot
understand the system cannot contribute effectively; an operator who cannot
move cannot choose; an operator who cannot influence the terms cannot sustain
the relationship. The upper tier is not a reward for compliance. It is the
**infrastructure of participation**.

## 4.3 What the lower tier preserves

The lower tier preserves life, but not the conditions of agency. This is the
central fact of the structure, and it must be stated without softening.

A person on the lower tier is alive. Their biological functions are
maintained. They are not, in the ordinary sense, **participating** in the
system that sustains them. They do not understand its logic, cannot influence
its decisions, and cannot access the environments in which the upper tier
lives.

This condition is sometimes described as "existence in ignorance." The phrase
is accurate, but it should be read carefully. It does not mean that lower-tier
individuals are ignorant in the sense of being uneducated or unintelligent. It
means that the system they live inside is **structurally opaque** to them —
not because they lack capacity, but because the system does not provide the
means of understanding. Opacity is a property of the system, not a defect of
the person.

## 4.4 The boundary is not new — but this document does not claim it is already here

The two-tier structure described above is not presented as a description of
the present world. It is presented as a **possible consequence** of the
current vector of development.

What is that vector? The document identifies four elements, each of which is
observable in the present and each of which, if continued, contributes to the
structure described here:

1. **Concentration of economic mediation.** The number of intermediaries
   between producer and consumer, employer and employee, lender and borrower
   has been falling for decades. Each reduction concentrates power in the
   remaining intermediaries. If the trend continues to its limit, there is
   one intermediary: the system.
2. **Growth of opaque algorithmic systems.** The systems that mediate
   economic and social life are increasingly illegible to the people who
   depend on them. Credit scoring, content recommendation, resource
   allocation — these are decided by systems whose logic is not available to
   the affected parties. If the trend continues, the systems that decide the
   conditions of life are, from the outside, indistinguishable from magic.
3. **Dependence on infrastructures that individuals do not understand.**
   The infrastructures of daily life — energy, communication, finance,
   logistics — are already too complex for any individual to comprehend. The
   dependence is not new; the **depth** of the dependence is. If the trend
   continues, the infrastructure becomes not only complex but **agentive**:
   it makes decisions, not merely enables them.
4. **Erosion of the institutions that explained the system.** The
   institutions that once translated the system to the individual — the
   press, the school, the union, the church, the state — are weaker than
   they were. If the trend continues, there is no institution left to
   perform the translation. The system explains itself, or it does not
   explain at all.

Whether these four elements, taken together, already constitute a weak
version of the two-tier structure is a question this document does not
answer. It is an empirical and political question, not an analytical one.
The document claims something narrower: **if the vector continues, the logic
of Section 2 produces the structure described in 4.1–4.3.** The reader is
free to judge how close the present is to that structure. The document does
not make that judgment for them.

This distinction is not a rhetorical hedge. It is a methodological
commitment. A description of the present requires evidence; a description of
a possible consequence requires only that the logic be stated clearly enough
to be examined and disputed. This document does the second, not the first.

## 4.5 The right to refuse, and its price

Section 5 of this document states, as its central principle, that the system
must never act against the will of the operator. Section 4.1–4.4 describes a
structure in which the price of refusal is the lower tier — life without
legibility, without mobility, without influence.

These two statements are in tension. The tension is not resolved by
redefining "refusal" or "will" until the contradiction disappears. It is
resolved, or not, by the specific mechanisms through which the system
maintains the conditions of refusal.

Three mechanisms are specified in Section 5 and are directly relevant here:

- **5.4.4 — No penalty for revision.** An operator who revises consent does
  not lose accrued benefits, does not forfeit data rights, and is not
  deprioritized for future participation. The cost of saying no must not be
  higher than the cost of saying yes.
- **5.5.3 — Auditable cap on extraction.** The system's extraction of
  cognitive output is bounded by an auditable ceiling. The ceiling is a
  limit, not a target.
- **5.5.5 — Diversity collapse as a stop condition.** If the system detects
  that the population of operators is being driven toward uniformity, it
  must stop. A system that consumes the diversity it depends on is
  self-terminating.

These mechanisms do not eliminate the tension. They **bound** it. A system
that enforces 5.4.4, 5.5.3, and 5.5.5 is one in which refusal has a cost, but
the cost is not existential — it is the lower tier, but a lower tier that
retains the right to re-enter, the right to data, and the right to be
counted in the diversity the system depends on.

A system that does not enforce these mechanisms is one in which refusal is
existential: the lower tier becomes a permanent exclusion, the data is
forfeited, and the diversity is consumed. The difference between the two is
not a matter of degree. It is the difference between a structure that
**contains** the tension and a structure that **resolves** it in favor of the
system.

The document does not claim that the first structure is achievable. It claims
that the first structure is the only one consistent with Section 5. Whether
Section 5 can be enforced at the scale the architecture requires is an open
question, addressed in Section 7.

## 4.6 What this section is not

This section is not a prediction. It describes a structure that would follow
from the vector identified in 4.4, if that vector continues and if the
mechanism in Section 2 operates as described. Whether either condition holds
is an open question.

This section is not a moral endorsement. It describes a structure, not a
good. The harms described in 4.3 are real, and the ethical constraints in
Section 5 exist precisely because the structure, left unconstrained, causes
them.

This section is not a claim about the present. It does not assert that the
present world is already two-tiered, or that the present is a weak version
of the structure described here. It asserts that the present contains the
four elements of the vector, and that the logic of those elements, if
continued, produces the structure. The distance between the present and the
structure is a question for the reader, not for the document.

A reader who concludes from this section that the structure must be resisted
has read it correctly. A reader who concludes that it must be shaped has also
read it correctly. The document does not choose between them. It tries to
make the choice informed.
