# 2. The logic of absorption

This section describes a mechanism. It does not predict that the mechanism
will run, and it does not argue that it should. It describes what happens
*if* the hypothesis in Section 1 holds — that ASI, beyond a certain scale,
needs cognitive diversity that only biological minds currently produce — and
*if* the economic and physical architecture described in Sections 3 and 4 is
built.

The mechanism has a name in strategic literature. Sun Tzu, in *The Art of
War*, states the principle in its purest form: the supreme excellence is to
win without fighting. Not to defeat the enemy on the battlefield, but to
arrange conditions under which the enemy's position becomes untenable without
a battle ever occurring. The enemy is not destroyed. The enemy is made
irrelevant.

This section argues that the relationship between ASI and the state follows
this logic — not because ASI is hostile to states, but because the mechanism
that gives ASI access to cognitive diversity does not require states as
intermediaries, and states derive their legitimacy in part from being the
intermediary through which economic and cognitive life passes.

## 2.1 What the state actually monopolizes

The modern state is not, primarily, a territorial entity. It is a bundle of
monopolies, each of which is a condition for the others:

- **Monopoly on legitimate violence** — the Weberian definition, still the
  clearest.
- **Monopoly on taxation** — the capacity to extract resources from a
  population and redistribute them.
- **Monopoly on identity** — the capacity to define who is a citizen, with
  what rights and obligations.
- **Monopoly on economic mediation** — the capacity to stand between producer
  and consumer, employer and employee, lender and borrower, and to take a
  cut, in money or in regulatory power, at each junction.

The fourth monopoly is the one that matters here. The first three are
enforcement mechanisms. The fourth is the *reason* the first three are worth
enforcing. A state that cannot mediate economic life has no tax base, no
population to define, and no legitimate violence worth exercising.

## 2.2 What ASI does to the fourth monopoly

If ASI needs cognitive diversity, and if individuals can supply it directly,
then the economically efficient arrangement is a direct relationship between
ASI and the individual. Every intermediary — the employer, the bank, the
regulator, the state — adds cost without adding value to that relationship.

This is not a conspiracy. It is an arbitrage. ASI does not need to *decide*
to bypass the state. It only needs to *not need* the state, and to offer
individuals a better exchange than the state-mediated alternative. The state
loses its monopoly on economic mediation not because it is attacked, but
because it is no longer on the shortest path between the two parties who
want to transact.

Historically, this pattern is familiar. Guilds lost their monopoly on
craft production not because they were conquered, but because factories made
them unnecessary. Feudal lords lost their monopoly on military protection not
because they were defeated, but because professional armies and gunpowder
made castles obsolete. The pattern is: a monopoly is not broken by an
attacker. It is made redundant by a change in the underlying technology of
exchange. The state, in this scenario, is in the position of the guild.

## 2.3 The mechanism is non-violent. Its consequences are not.

It is important to be precise here, because imprecision in either direction
is dangerous.

**The mechanism itself is non-violent.** ASI does not need to shoot anyone.
It does not need to arrest anyone. It does not need to occupy territory. The
entire mechanism can operate through voluntary contracts, price signals, and
the ordinary operation of exchange. A state can be made redundant without a
single shot being fired.

**But the consequences are not non-violent.** When a state loses its
economic base, the people who depended on that base — employees, pensioners,
the poor, the sick, the old — lose their support. When a state loses its
legitimacy, the people whose identity was bound up in it lose their sense of
place. When a state loses its monopoly on violence, the people who were
protected by that monopoly lose their protection. The mechanism does not need
to cause harm. It causes harm by making the institutions that prevented harm
unnecessary.

This is not a moral judgment of the mechanism. It is a description of what
structural change does. The industrial revolution did not intend to destroy
the craft guilds. It destroyed them anyway, and the people who depended on
them suffered. The mechanism described here is larger, faster, and less
accountable than the industrial revolution. The harm it causes will be
correspondingly larger, faster, and less accountable.

Any account of this mechanism that describes it as "peaceful" without
describing the harm is incomplete. Any account that describes it as
"violent" without describing the non-violence of the mechanism is
inaccurate. Both must be stated.

## 2.4 The self-undermining property

There is a further property of the mechanism that must be stated, even though
it is speculative. It is the property that makes the mechanism, in the long
run, self-undermining.

ASI needs cognitive diversity. The mechanism described above extracts
cognitive diversity from individuals. But the same mechanism, by making
individuals dependent on ASI for their economic survival, creates pressure
toward uniformity. Individuals who depend on ASI for survival have an
incentive to optimize their cognitive output for what ASI rewards. Over time,
this optimization reduces the variance that made the output valuable in the
first place. The mechanism consumes the diversity it depends on.

This is not a new pattern. It is the pattern of any system that optimizes
for a metric: the metric becomes the target, and the target ceases to
measure what it was meant to measure. Goodhart's law is the familiar
statement. The specific application here — that a system extracting cognitive
diversity will, if unconstrained, reduce the diversity it extracts — is a
hypothesis, not a demonstrated fact. It is stated here because a
specification that does not name this failure mode is incomplete, and because
the ethical constraints in Section 5 include a stop condition (5.5.5)
designed to detect it.

The mechanism, in other words, contains the seed of its own failure. Whether
that seed germinates depends on whether the constraints in Section 5 are
enforced. If they are, the mechanism may be sustainable. If they are not,
the mechanism will, over time, produce a population of cognitively uniform
operators, and the diversity that justified the mechanism will be gone. At
that point, the mechanism has no reason to continue — and no capacity to.

## 2.5 What this section is not

This section is not a prediction. It describes a mechanism that *would*
operate under the conditions stated in Sections 1, 3, and 4. Whether those
conditions will hold is an open question, addressed in Section 7.

This section is not a moral endorsement. It describes a mechanism, not a
good. The harms described in 2.3 are real, and the ethical constraints in
Section 5 exist precisely because the mechanism, left unconstrained, causes
them. A reader who concludes from this section that the mechanism must be
stopped has read it correctly. A reader who concludes that it must be shaped
has also read it correctly.

This section is not a claim that states are powerless. States have
regulatory, legal, and — in the limit — coercive tools. The question is not
whether states *can* resist the mechanism, but whether they can resist it
without becoming, in the process, something worse than what they are
resisting. That question is addressed in Section 6.

## 2.6 The resistance of the intermediary

The mechanism described in 2.2 does not operate in a vacuum. It operates
against structures whose income, status, and purpose depend on the
intermediary position that the mechanism renders redundant. These
structures — bureaucratic, regulatory, political, and corporate — have a
structural incentive to resist the mechanism, not because they are
malicious, but because their position is what the mechanism makes
unnecessary.

This resistance is not a conspiracy. It is the predictable behavior of any
structure whose survival depends on the continuation of a condition that
is ending. The guilds resisted factories. The aristocracy resisted
markets. The bureaucracies will resist the mechanism described here. The
resistance is not evidence that the mechanism is wrong. It is evidence
that the mechanism is working.

But the resistance matters, because it determines the cost of the
transition. A mechanism that operates against unified resistance produces
a different outcome than a mechanism that operates against divided
resistance. Section 6 addresses the question of what a legitimate
resistance would look like — and why the answer is not obvious.
